# Haploinsufficiency genes by CHatGPT

**Haploinsufficiency (HI)** is a genetic mechanism in which one functional copy of a gene is insufficient to maintain 
normal biological function, so loss of function in the other copy causes disease.

This is one of the most important mechanisms in Mendelian genetics, particularly in autosomal dominant disorders.

The scenario you describe is typically:

- One allele carries a premature termination codon (PTC), caused by a nonsense variant or an upstream frameshift.
- The other allele is intact and functional.
- The mutant allele produces little or no functional protein, often because of nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD).
- The remaining functional allele cannot produce enough protein to maintain normal function.
- The resulting phenotype is typically autosomal dominant.

**Importantly, not every early stop codon causes haploinsufficiency or disease.** The effect depends on the gene, transcript, position of the variant, and whether the truncated protein is degraded or has a dominant-negative or gain-of-function effect.

## 3. The important distinction: haploinsufficiency versus dominant-negative effects

For your specific question, this distinction is crucial.

| Mechanism | What happens to the mutant allele? | Why disease occurs |
|---|---|---|
| Haploinsufficiency | Mutant protein absent or nonfunctional | One normal copy is insufficient |
| Dominant-negative | Mutant protein is produced | Mutant protein interferes with the normal protein |
| Gain-of-function | Mutant protein is produced | Mutant protein acquires abnormal activity |
| Loss of function, recessive | Mutant protein absent or nonfunctional | One normal copy is sufficient |

For a typical haploinsufficient gene, a premature stop codon that causes NMD is often the clearest pathogenic mechanism.

However, **a premature stop codon is not automatically equivalent to a null allele**.

### Why stop-codon position matters

The classic rule is that a premature termination codon more than approximately 50–55 nucleotides upstream of the final exon-exon junction is likely to trigger NMD.

Exceptions include:

- Variants in the final exon
- Variants near the end of the penultimate exon
- Alternative transcripts
- Alternative translation initiation
- Tissue-specific transcript expression
- Genes with unusual NMD behavior

A terminal truncation may produce a stable protein with residual or abnormal activity rather than a true null allele.

ClinGen explicitly recommends considering transcript structure, NMD prediction, and the possibility of residual protein function when evaluating LoF variants. [clinicalgenome.org](https://www.clinicalgenome.org/site/assets/files/6428/dosage_sop-scoring-1.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## 4. Where to get a curated list of haploinsufficient genes

For a computational biology workflow, I would not manually maintain the gene list above. I would use a curated resource.

### ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity Map

The most directly relevant resource is the **Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen) Dosage Sensitivity Map**.

[ClinGen Dosage Sensitivity Map](https://search.clinicalgenome.org/kb/gene-dosage?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

It provides gene-level haploinsufficiency classifications:

| HI score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 3 | Sufficient evidence for haploinsufficiency |
| 2 | Emerging evidence |
| 1 | Little evidence |
| 0 | No evidence |
| 30 | Associated with autosomal recessive disease |
| 40 | Dosage sensitivity unlikely |

**For a high-confidence gene set, I would start with HI score 3.** This is ClinGen's strongest evidence category. [clinicalgenome.org](https://www.clinicalgenome.org/tools/cnv-webinar/faq/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

ClinGen also provides downloadable curation files, which are useful for integrating these annotations into variant prioritization pipelines. [ClinGen](https://search.clinicalgenome.org/kb/downloads?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## 5. If your goal is to identify pathogenic heterozygous stop variants computationally

I would construct a gene-level filter along these lines:

1. **ClinGen HI score = 3**
2. LoF is an established mechanism for the specific disease
3. Variant is heterozygous
4. Variant is in a clinically relevant transcript
5. Variant is predicted to cause NMD
6. Variant is not rescued by alternative splicing or transcript structure
7. Variant is not in a gene where LoF causes disease through a different mechanism
8. Variant-level evidence supports pathogenicity

This would be a reasonable starting point for a dominant LoF variant prioritization pipeline.

For variant interpretation, the ACMG/AMP PVS1 criterion is particularly relevant: it applies to null variants in genes where LoF is an established disease mechanism, with strength adjusted according to variant-specific considerations. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9980257/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### A final conceptual point

If you're investigating this from a genomic or evolutionary perspective, there is an interesting asymmetry:

**Haploinsufficient genes tend to be under strong purifying selection**, because heterozygous LoF variants themselves can reduce fitness.

This is reflected in population constraint metrics such as pLI and LOEUF, but those metrics capture related rather than identical biological properties.

For a systematic analysis, combining ClinGen HI classifications with gnomAD LoF constraint, transcript-aware variant annotation, and ClinVar pathogenicity would be much more informative than using any single source.

The resulting dataset could distinguish genes with established dominant haploinsufficiency from genes that are merely intolerant to LoF variation. That distinction is where a lot of the interesting biology lives.
